Anyone can write `From: hi@acme.com` on a mail. SMTP does not check it. So Gmail, Outlook and the other mailbox providers look at three DNS records to know if a mail really comes from `acme.com`: SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

## SPF: which servers can send

SPF (Sender Policy Framework) is a TXT record that lists the servers that can send mail for a domain:

```txt
v=spf1 include:_spf.example.net -all
```

The receiving server reads the domain of the **envelope sender** (the `MAIL FROM` of the SMTP talk, also called the return path), finds its SPF record, and checks the IP address that connects. If the IP is not on the list, SPF fails.

SPF has two limits:

- It checks the envelope sender, not the `From` that the person sees.
- It breaks when a mail is forwarded, because the forwarder has a new IP.

## DKIM: a signature on the mail

DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) signs each mail. The sending server makes a hash of the body and of some headers, and signs it with a private key. It adds the result as a `DKIM-Signature` header:

```txt
DKIM-Signature: v=1; a=rsa-sha256; d=acme.com; s=1billion; h=From:To:Subject:Date; bh=…; b=…
```

`d` is the domain and `s` is the selector. The receiving server reads the public key at `<selector>._domainkey.<domain>`, and checks the signature. If someone changes the body or a signed header on the way, DKIM fails.

A DKIM signature stays on the mail when it is forwarded. This is why DKIM is the strongest of the three.

## DMARC: the rule that joins them

SPF and DKIM do not look at the `From` that the person sees. DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance) closes that gap. It is a TXT record at `_dmarc.<domain>`:

```txt
v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc@acme.com
```

DMARC passes when SPF or DKIM passes **and** its domain matches the domain of `From`. This match is called *alignment*. With relaxed alignment (the default), a subdomain also matches: `mail.acme.com` aligns with `acme.com`.

`p` tells the receiver what to do with a mail that fails:

| `p` | What the receiver does |
|---|---|
| `none` | Nothing. It only sends reports to `rua`. |
| `quarantine` | It puts the mail in spam. |
| `reject` | It refuses the mail. |

Start with `p=none`, read the reports, and go to `quarantine` or `reject` when all of your senders pass.

## How 1billion sets them up

When you add a domain to an app in the console, it shows these records:

| Type | Name | What |
|---|---|---|
| TXT | `1billion._domainkey.<domain>` | The DKIM public key |
| CNAME | `1billion.<domain>` | Our return path, which holds the SPF and the MX |
| TXT | `_dmarc.<domain>` | A DMARC policy, for example `v=DMARC1; p=none;` |

- **DKIM:** 1billion signs each mail with the private key of your domain. We keep that key encrypted, and it never leaves our servers.
- **SPF:** the envelope sender is `bounces+<message id>@1billion.<your domain>`. The CNAME points that name to our host, so SPF passes, and it aligns with your domain. When our servers change, only our records change: you never edit your DNS again.
- **DMARC:** it is not required, but mailbox providers trust a domain with DMARC more. The console shows a warning when it is missing.

The console checks the records for you, and the domain can send when DKIM, SPF and MX pass. The full table is in [Domains and DNS](/docs/guides/domains).
